\begin{abstract}
We present \system, a hierarchical retrieval-augmented generation (RAG)
framework designed for scene-level understanding of long-form video content.
A fundamental challenge in video question answering is that relevant evidence
is heterogeneous in nature, spanning visual keyframes, spoken dialogue,
character metadata, and narrative context---modalities that existing
single-index pipelines cannot simultaneously address.  \system\ resolves this
by treating each scene as a structured, knowledge-bearing unit and organizing
its metadata into a principled five-layer schema (L0--L5), ranging from raw
visual embeddings to high-level narrative relations.

Our technical contributions are threefold.  First, we propose the
\textbf{layered scene schema} (L0--L5), a unified data contract that
integrates CLIP visual embeddings~\cite{clip}, temporal anchors,
VLM-generated semantic descriptions~\cite{llava,blip2}, Whisper speech
transcripts~\cite{whisper}, character metadata, and narrative relations into a
single JSON-structured scene chunk.  Second, we design a \textbf{four-tier
FAISS indexing pipeline}~\cite{faiss}: a 94\,779-vector frame-level visual
index (CLIP ViT-B/32), a 12\,900-vector scene-level fusion index (72\% CLIP
+ 28\% SentenceTransformer~\cite{sbert}, 896-dim), a 12\,900-vector text
knowledge index (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384-dim), and a dedicated 33\,157-vector
ActivityNet~\cite{activitynet} activity index.  Third, we implement an
\textbf{agentic retrieval pipeline} that performs query decomposition,
multi-index evidence aggregation, and LLM-based answer verification.

The system has been built and evaluated at \textbf{993-movie + 815-video
scale}: 53 movies with full five-layer annotation, 940 trailer-sourced movies
fully enriched at the L2 semantic layer via Groq Llama-4-Scout, and 815
ActivityNet videos with 100\% Whisper STT coverage.  On the 53-movie
full-annotation corpus the text knowledge index achieves
\textbf{R@1\,=\,92.8\%} and \textbf{MRR\,=\,0.794} on character retrieval
with 90.1\% dialogue coverage.  The end-to-end QA benchmark on 12\,900
chunks records R@1\,=\,\textbf{86.8\%} on scene description (T1),
\textbf{61.3\%} on character retrieval (T2, up from 48.8\% after Whisper
integration), 5.6\% on dialogue grounding (T3), and 29.3\% on visual setting
search (T4).  The CLIP visual index achieves L0 R@1\,=\,50.4\%
(MRR\,=\,0.558).  All components---visual benchmarking, DeepFace face
detection, ActivityNet VLM enrichment, multi-hop Neo4j graph reasoning, and
grounded answer generation---are fully operational.
\end{abstract}

\small \textbf{Keywords:} Video understaning, clip, vision language models, rag, multi-model retrieval

\section{Introduction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Understanding long-form video content at the \emph{scene level} is an open
and challenging problem in multimedia information retrieval and natural
language processing.  Videos are inherently multi-modal, combining visual
appearance, spoken language, temporal structure, and narrative coherence in
ways that no single modality can fully characterize.  Existing
retrieval-augmented approaches typically address only a subset of this
richness: frame-level image similarity search~\cite{clip,openclip},
caption-based dense retrieval~\cite{dpr,rag}, or short-clip
grounding~\cite{vidore,videorag}.  Although each of these strategies is
effective within its own scope, they fall short when users pose the kinds of
scene-level questions that arise in practice---\emph{``Which scene best
illustrates the conflict between two characters?''} or \emph{``Find the scene
in which the protagonist delivers the final monologue in the
courtroom.''} Answering such queries requires joint reasoning over visual
appearance, dialogue, character identity, and narrative context
simultaneously.

This gap motivates a new framing of video understanding as \emph{structured
retrieval over semantically enriched scene units}.  Rather than treating a
video as an undifferentiated sequence of frames or a bag of captions, we
propose representing each scene as a \emph{knowledge-bearing unit} annotated
with layered, heterogeneous metadata.  On top of this representation, we
construct a retrieval-augmented generation system, \system, that supports
both direct fact-lookup queries and interpretive, multi-hop reasoning.

The technical challenge is threefold.  (i)~\textbf{Representation}: How
should a scene be structured so that different modalities can be stored,
indexed, and retrieved independently yet coherently?  (ii)~\textbf{Indexing}:
Given the diversity of query types (visual, textual, character-centric,
dialogue-grounded), how should multiple indexes be organized so that each
query type is routed to the most appropriate retrieval signal?
(iii)~\textbf{Retrieval and generation}: How can evidence from multiple
modalities and indexes be aggregated and used to produce grounded, verifiable
answers?

\system\ addresses all three challenges through a unified architecture.  Our
specific contributions are:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item A \textbf{five-layer scene schema} (L0--L5) decomposing video scenes
        into temporal anchors, CLIP visual embeddings, VLM-generated semantic
        descriptions, Whisper dialogue transcripts, cast-and-emotion metadata,
        and narrative relations---forming a common data contract for all
        downstream retrieval and reasoning components.
  \item A \textbf{four-tier FAISS indexing pipeline}: a 94\,779-vector
        keyframe visual index (CLIP ViT-B/32, including 33\,157 ActivityNet
        vectors), a 12\,900-vector L1 fusion index
        ($\sqrt{0.72}\,\mathbf{c}\oplus\sqrt{0.28}\,\mathbf{t}$, 896-dim), a
        12\,900-vector knowledge text index (all-MiniLM-L6-v2, 384-dim), and
        a dedicated 33\,157-vector ActivityNet activity index.
  \item An \textbf{agentic retrieval pipeline} with five functional modules:
        query decomposition, multi-level retrieval, evidence aggregation,
        grounded answer generation (Groq LLM), and an LLM-based answer
        verifier.
  \item \textbf{Empirical evaluation} at 993-movie + 815-video scale, with a
        1\,050-query end-to-end QA benchmark spanning four task types, a visual
        retrieval benchmark on 94\,779 CLIP vectors, and a multi-hop graph
        reasoning evaluation on 10\,792 Neo4j nodes.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows.  Section~\ref{sec:related}
reviews related work.  Section~\ref{sec:schema} describes the layered scene
representation.  Section~\ref{sec:index} presents the hierarchical indexing
architecture.  Section~\ref{sec:agent} details the agentic retrieval pipeline.
Section~\ref{sec:data} describes the dataset and implementation.
Section~\ref{sec:eval} reports experimental results.  Section~\ref{sec:discussion}
discusses strengths, limitations, and positioning.
Section~\ref{sec:future} outlines future directions, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Retrieval-Augmented Generation.}
Lewis et al.~\cite{rag} introduced RAG as a mechanism for grounding large
language model outputs in retrieved, non-parametric knowledge.  Subsequent
work has extended the framework to open-book question answering~\cite{fid},
language model pre-training~\cite{realm}, and long-context
generation~\cite{retro}.  More recent efforts combine dense retrieval with
LLM-based self-reflection~\cite{selfrag} and active retrieval
triggering~\cite{flare}, achieving improved faithfulness on multi-hop queries.
\system\ follows this line of work but targets the multimodal,
scene-structured domain, requiring dedicated visual and graph-based retrieval
stages in addition to standard dense text retrieval.

\paragraph{Dense Passage Retrieval.}
Karpukhin et al.~\cite{dpr} demonstrated that bi-encoder models trained with
contrastive objectives substantially outperform sparse BM25 retrieval on
open-domain QA\@.  ColBERT~\cite{colbert} introduced late interaction to
provide fine-grained token-level matching within the efficiency constraints of
dense retrieval.  SentenceTransformers~\cite{sbert} provide efficient
sentence-level embeddings widely adopted for semantic similarity and passage
retrieval.  \system\ adopts the all-MiniLM-L6-v2 SentenceTransformer for its
L3 Knowledge Text Index, benefiting from the generalizability of bi-encoder
representations at inference-time efficiency~\cite{ir_book}.

\paragraph{Vision-Language Models and Multi-Modal Retrieval.}
CLIP~\cite{clip} established the paradigm of aligning image and text
representations through contrastive pre-training on web-scale data, enabling
zero-shot visual retrieval.  OpenCLIP~\cite{openclip} scaled this approach to
larger architectures and datasets.  BLIP~\cite{blip} and BLIP-2~\cite{blip2}
extended vision-language alignment to generation tasks, while
Flamingo~\cite{flamingo} introduced few-shot multi-modal learning.
LLaVA~\cite{llava} proposed an efficient visual instruction tuning approach
for open-ended visual question answering.  \system\ leverages CLIP ViT-B/32
for keyframe embedding and Groq Llama-4-Scout VLMs for automated semantic
scene description generation.

\paragraph{Video Understanding and Retrieval.}
Early work on video understanding focused on action recognition~\cite{activitynet,two_stream}
and dense video captioning~\cite{dense_cap}.  VideoBERT~\cite{videobert}
pioneered joint video-text pre-training, while InternVideo2~\cite{internvideo2}
adopts large-scale transformer architectures for multimodal video
representation.  For video retrieval, VideoRAG~\cite{videorag} proposes a RAG
pipeline over video corpora, and ColPali~\cite{vidore} demonstrates the value
of multi-granularity visual representations for document retrieval.
Scene-level understanding has been explored through MovieNet~\cite{movienet}
and MovieGraphs~\cite{moviegraphs}, which provide structured annotations
including shot boundaries, character interactions, and causal relations.
\system\ builds directly on these datasets for its Tier~1 full-annotation
corpus.

\paragraph{Speech Recognition for Video Grounding.}
Automatic speech recognition (ASR) has become a standard preprocessing step
for video question answering.  Whisper~\cite{whisper} provides a robust,
large-scale multilingual ASR model with strong generalization across domains.
Several recent video QA systems~\cite{videorag,internvideo2} incorporate ASR
transcripts to supplement visual features with dialogue-level evidence.  Our
work demonstrates that Whisper transcripts improve T2 character retrieval by
+12.5 R@1 points, even without time-aligned scene-level segmentation.

\paragraph{Knowledge Graphs for Video Understanding.}
Graph-based representations including scene graphs~\cite{scene_graph} and
narrative graphs~\cite{moviegraphs} support multi-hop reasoning beyond what
dense retrieval alone can provide.  Neo4j~\cite{neo4j} has been widely used
as a graph database backend for relational knowledge storage.  \system\
instantiates a Neo4j narrative graph covering 58 movies and 10\,792+ nodes,
supporting relational queries such as co-character appearance chains, causal
scene links, and narrative arc traversal.

\paragraph{Face Recognition in Video.}
Face detection and recognition in video have been studied
extensively~\cite{deepface,retinaface}.  Character identification in films
remains challenging due to lighting variation, occlusion, and the absence of
labeled reference databases for most titles~\cite{movienet}.  We incorporate
DeepFace RetinaFace~\cite{retinaface} for L4 face detection, enriching
1\,377 trailer chunks with face counts, leaving full identity assignment for
future work.

\section{Layered Scene Representation}
\label{sec:schema}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Design Rationale}

A scene in a long-form video is far richer than any single embedding can
capture.  Different retrieval tasks draw on different kinds of evidence: a
character-centric query relies on names and co-occurrence statistics, a visual
setting query relies on keyframe appearance, and a dialogue grounding query
requires exact spoken text.  Encoding all of these signals into a single
vector necessarily dilutes each individual signal.

\system\ addresses this by decoupling scene \emph{representation} from scene
\emph{retrieval}.  At the representation level, each scene is stored as a
structured JSON chunk carrying metadata across six complementary layers.  At
the retrieval level, multiple independent indexes are maintained, each
optimized for a different subset of these layers.  This separation allows the
system to route different query types to the most appropriate index without
losing information from the other layers.

Table~\ref{tab:layers}.  Each scene chunk is a JSON object that carries all
six layers, with missing fields populated by automated enrichment pipelines
(VLM, Whisper STT, TMDB API) where source annotations are unavailable.

\section{Hierarchical Indexing Architecture}
\label{sec:index}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Motivation}

A single index cannot efficiently serve all retrieval task types present in
long-form video QA\@.  Character retrieval depends primarily on named entity
signals in L4, which are best encoded by a text embedding model.  Visual
setting retrieval depends on L0 keyframe appearance, requiring a visual
embedding model.  Dialogue-grounded retrieval requires exact phrase matching
in L3, which benefits from dedicated text encoding without interference from
other fields.  A hierarchical, multi-index architecture allows each query type
to be routed to the index in which its primary signal is strongest, while
optional cross-index fusion provides evidence from secondary modalities where
useful.

database, as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:index_levels}.

\subsection{Fusion Weighting}

The L1 Scene Fusion Index combines visual and textual representations via a
weighted concatenation:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{v}_\text{fusion}
  = \mathrm{normalize}\!\left(\left[
      \sqrt{0.72}\;\mathbf{c} \;\Big\|\; \sqrt{0.28}\;\mathbf{t}
    \right]\right),
  \quad \mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{R}^{512},\;\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{384},
  \label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{c}$ is the L2-normalized mean CLIP embedding over all
keyframes in the scene and $\mathbf{t}$ is the L2-normalized
SentenceTransformer embedding of the L2 semantic description.  The
square-root weights ensure that each sub-vector's contribution to the inner
product score is proportional to the intended weight (72\% visual,
28\% textual)~\cite{faiss}.  The 72/28 split preserves the discriminability
of visual features while incorporating sufficient semantic grounding to reduce
cross-movie visual confusion.

\section{Agentic Retrieval Pipeline}
\label{sec:agent}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Motivation}

A monolithic query-to-answer pipeline conflates retrieval and generation,
making it difficult to attribute errors (retrieval failure vs.\ generation
hallucination), to extend the system to new index types, or to enforce factual
grounding of generated answers.  The agentic formulation~\cite{react,toolformer}
addresses these limitations by decomposing the pipeline into explicitly
modular, inspectable stages.  Each stage exposes its inputs and outputs,
enabling independent debugging, evaluation, and improvement.  Moreover, the
agentic design naturally supports \emph{hybrid retrieval strategies}: for
example, a dialogue-first retrieval pass can be followed by visual scene
reranking, a strategy that would be opaque in a monolithic pipeline.

The agentic pipeline consists of five functional modules, described in
Table~\ref{tab:agents}.

\centering
\begin{tabular}{%

  >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{9.0cm}}
\toprule

\midrule
Query Decomposer  &

  instructions. \\[4pt]

  abstention. \\
\bottomrule

\subsection{Data Processing Pipeline}

\paragraph{Tier~1 Pipeline.}
Scene boundaries are extracted from MovieNet~\cite{movienet} structural
annotations.  SRT dialogue is aligned using a timestamp-overlap matcher that
assigns subtitle segments to the scene chunk with maximum temporal overlap,
yielding 90.1\% coverage across the 53-movie corpus.  Visual descriptions,
setting tags, action labels, and emotional tone are generated by Groq
Llama-4-Scout VLM, batched at four keyframes per API call.  Character
metadata is populated from MovieGraphs~\cite{moviegraphs} and TMDB cast API
queries.

\paragraph{Tier~2 Pipeline.}
{\sloppy The pipeline is fully automated.  yt-dlp queries YouTube for
\texttt{``\{title\} official trailer''} and downloads at $\leq$480p (max
200\,MB); ffmpeg extracts one keyframe per 3\,s at $224\times224$; OpenCLIP
ViT-B/32~\cite{openclip} encodes each frame to a 512-dim L2-normalized
embedding; per-movie \texttt{IndexFlatIP} indexes are built and merged into
the global \texttt{visual\_index.faiss}.  All 4\,588 trailer chunks receive
Groq LLM L2 enrichment (100\% coverage).\par}

\paragraph{Tier~3 Pipeline.}
ActivityNet~\cite{activitynet} videos are processed via the same CLIP
pipeline as Tier~2, producing 33\,157 keyframe vectors across 3\,687 chunks.
Whisper STT~\cite{whisper} is applied to all 2\,576 audio tracks via the
Groq API with multi-key rate-limit rotation, achieving 100\% transcript
coverage.  All 3\,687 chunks additionally receive L2 semantic enrichment via
Groq LLM (100\% coverage).

\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

We report results across two complementary evaluation suites.
\textbf{Internal metrics (M1--M4)} measure index quality on the 53-movie
Tier~1 corpus (4\,625 chunks).  \textbf{End-to-end QA tasks (T1--T4)}
measure retrieval accuracy over the full 12\,900-chunk index using a
held-out benchmark of 1\,050 queries, covering the full range of query types
supported by the system.  All tasks are evaluated using Recall at rank $k$
(R@$k$) and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR), following standard information
retrieval conventions~\cite{ir_book}.

\paragraph{Internal Metrics.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{M1 — Nearest-Neighbor Consistency:} Mean cosine similarity
        of the top-1 intra-movie neighbor for each chunk.
  \item \textbf{M2 — Character Retrieval:} R@1 and MRR over 1\,950 unique
        character name queries across 53 movies.
  \item \textbf{M3 — Cross-Movie Confusion:} Fraction of chunks whose top-20
        neighbors include at least one chunk from a different movie.
  \item \textbf{M4 — SRT Dialogue Coverage:} Fraction of Tier~1 chunks with
        non-empty \texttt{dialogue\_text} after SRT alignment.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{End-to-End QA Tasks.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{T1 — Scene Description:} 250 queries matching a
        natural-language scene description.
  \item \textbf{T2 — Character Retrieval:} 400 queries asking for scenes
        containing a specified character.
  \item \textbf{T3 — Dialogue Grounding:} 250 queries containing a quoted
        dialogue fragment to be matched.
  \item \textbf{T4 — Visual Setting Search:} 150 queries describing a visual
        setting (e.g., ``outdoor night scene'').
\end{itemize}

The Whisper STT integration produces clear task-dependent effects.  For T2
character retrieval, the improvement is substantial: R@1 rises from 48.8\%
to \textbf{61.3\%} (MRR: 0.676) after 720 ActivityNet chunks receive dialogue

This confirms that character names frequently appear in spoken dialogue, and
that Whisper provides a practical, scalable source of this signal~\cite{whisper}.
The minor T1 regression ($-$1.3 R@1 points) is attributable to the absence
of time-aligned Whisper segmentation: full-video transcripts span multiple
scene chunks, introducing cross-scene noise that reduces per-chunk retrieval
precision.  This limitation is discussed further in Section~\ref{sec:future}.

\subsection{Discussion of Results}

The 92.8\% R@1 character retrieval result validates the L4 metadata layer as
the most discriminative retrieval signal in the schema and confirms that the
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 encoder~\cite{sbert} provides strong semantic alignment for
named entity queries.  The 51.3\% cross-movie confusion rate in M3 reflects
thematic similarity among films in the 53-movie corpus (e.g., multiple crime
dramas sharing character archetypes), motivating the L1 fusion index's
incorporation of CLIP visual features to reduce false positives.  The 90.1\%
SRT coverage validates the timestamp-overlap alignment strategy as a practical
and robust approach to subtitle-to-scene assignment.

The most significant finding is the T3 dialogue grounding gap (5.6\% R@1).
In the canonical multi-field encoding, \texttt{dialogue\_text} competes with
seven other fields, diluting the exact-phrase matching signal that dialogue
queries depend on.  This analysis motivates the primary architectural extension
proposed in Section~\ref{sec:future}: a dedicated dialogue-only index combined
with the main knowledge index via late fusion~\cite{colbert,dpr}.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\subsection{Strengths}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Scale and coverage.}  The system indexes 993 unique movie
        titles and 815 ActivityNet videos, with 100\% Whisper STT coverage
        across 2\,576 ActivityNet audio tracks and 100\% VLM L2 enrichment
        across all three corpus tiers.

  \item \textbf{Multi-modal heterogeneity.}  Four independent FAISS
        indexes~\cite{faiss}---visual (94\,779), fusion (12\,900), text
        (12\,900), and ActivityNet (33\,157) vectors---cover complementary
        retrieval strategies without requiring a single large multi-modal
        model.  Each index can be improved, replaced, or extended
        independently.

  \item \textbf{Practical speech integration.}  The 90.1\% SRT dialogue
        coverage via timestamp-overlap alignment and 100\% Whisper~\cite{whisper}
        coverage for ActivityNet demonstrate two complementary,
        production-ready speech-to-text integration approaches.

  \item \textbf{Empirically validated improvement.}  The T2 character
        retrieval gain from 48.8\% to 61.3\% R@1 after Whisper integration
        provides a clean empirical demonstration of the value of
        dialogue-level evidence for character-centric queries, consistent with
        findings in multi-modal video QA~\cite{videorag,internvideo2}.

  \item \textbf{Complete evaluation suite.}  Five distinct evaluation
        benchmarks (1\,050-query QA, internal character retrieval, visual
        retrieval, dialogue coverage, and multi-hop graph reasoning) enable
        fine-grained diagnosis of component-level limitations.

  \item \textbf{Modular schema design.}  The L0--L5 layered representation
        decouples data collection, indexing, and retrieval, allowing each
        layer to evolve independently---mirroring best practices in modular
        NLP system design~\cite{rag,selfrag}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Dialogue grounding (T3).}  R@1\,=\,5.6\% is the system's
        primary weak point, caused by signal dilution in the canonical
        multi-field encoding.  A dedicated dialogue-only FAISS index combined
        with the main index via late fusion~\cite{colbert,dpr} would directly
        address this without sacrificing T1/T2/T4 performance.

  \item \textbf{Visual retrieval cross-movie ambiguity.}  L0 CLIP achieves
        50.4\% R@1 with a 49.6\% cross-movie confusion rate.  Visually generic
        scene types (outdoor landscapes, interior dialogue shots) are
        inherently difficult to disambiguate using visual appearance
        alone~\cite{clip,openclip}; the L1 fusion index partially mitigates
        this.

  \item \textbf{Face identity assignment.}  DeepFace RetinaFace~\cite{retinaface}
        detects face regions in 1\,377 trailer chunks but cannot assign actor
        identities without a per-movie reference face database.  The absence
        of cached TMDB cast images limits the L4 \texttt{cast\_in\_scene}
        field to face count statistics.

  \item \textbf{Whisper temporal alignment.}  Whisper transcripts are
        full-video and distributed across multiple scene chunks without
        time-aligned segmentation, causing cross-scene noise that explains the
        minor T1 regression observed after Whisper integration.

  \item \textbf{Copyright constraints.}  Twelve Tier~1 movies lack local
        MP4 files, preventing Whisper STT for these titles and leaving a
        residual 9.9\% gap in Tier~1 dialogue coverage.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{System Positioning}

\system\ is best characterized as a \emph{systems-level contribution}: a
complete, reproducible multi-modal RAG pipeline that achieves strong retrieval
performance across diverse query types at nearly 1\,000-movie scale.  The
system's primary novelty lies in the integration of a structured scene
representation (L0--L5 schema), heterogeneous FAISS indexing, automated VLM
and ASR enrichment pipelines, and graph-based relational reasoning into a
unified, end-to-end operational system.  The empirical results---T1 R@1\,=\,86.8\%,
T2 R@1\,=\,61.3\%, L0 visual R@1\,=\,50.4\%, multi-hop graph reasoning
across 10\,792 Neo4j nodes---collectively demonstrate that the integrated
system substantially outperforms any single component in isolation.

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Future Work}
\label{sec:future}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Having demonstrated the effectiveness and scalability of the core architecture,
we identify five high-priority extensions that address the system's current
limitations.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Dedicated dialogue index with late fusion.}  Building a
        separate FAISS index encoding only \texttt{dialogue\_text} via a
        speech-language encoder~\cite{sbert} and combining it with the main
        knowledge index via score-level late fusion~\cite{colbert,dpr} would
        directly address the T3 dialogue grounding limitation without degrading
        T1/T2/T4 performance.

  \item \textbf{Face identity matching via TMDB cast images.}  The DeepFace
        RetinaFace~\cite{retinaface} detection pipeline enriches 1\,377 trailer
        chunks with face count metadata.  Caching TMDB cast headshots and
        computing reference embeddings would complete the L4
        \texttt{cast\_in\_scene} field for identity-level character queries.

  \item \textbf{L1 Fusion Index visual evaluation.}  The 12\,900-vector L1
        Fusion Index has not yet been evaluated with image query vectors.  A
        direct comparison between L0 (CLIP-only, 50.4\% R@1) and L1 (fusion)
        would quantify the disambiguation benefit of visual-textual fusion.

  \item \textbf{Time-aligned Whisper segmentation.}  Assigning to each scene
        chunk only the transcript words whose timestamps fall within the
        chunk's temporal window would recover the per-chunk precision currently
        lost to cross-scene noise, resolving the minor T1/T2 regressions
        observed after Whisper integration.

  \item \textbf{LLM-to-Cypher translation.}  The current graph reasoning
        module uses six hand-crafted Cypher templates~\cite{neo4j}.
        Replacing these with a learnable LLM-to-Cypher translation
        layer~\cite{react,toolformer} would generalize multi-hop graph
        reasoning to arbitrary natural-language queries.
\end{itemize}

% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We presented \system, a hierarchical retrieval-augmented generation framework
for scene-level video understanding, evaluated at \textbf{993-movie +
815-video scale}.  The system integrates a principled five-layer scene
representation schema, four operational FAISS indexes (94\,779-vector visual,
12\,900-vector fusion, 12\,900-vector text knowledge, and 33\,157-vector
ActivityNet activity indexes), a Neo4j narrative graph covering 58 movies and
10\,792+ nodes, and a five-module agentic retrieval pipeline into a unified,
reproducible end-to-end architecture.

On the 53-movie full-annotation corpus, the text knowledge index achieves
R@1\,=\,92.8\% and MRR\,=\,0.794 on character retrieval with 90.1\% SRT
dialogue coverage.  The 1\,050-query end-to-end QA benchmark demonstrates a
clear retrieval hierarchy: scene description (T1) achieves 86.8\% R@1,
character retrieval (T2) reaches 61.3\% R@1---a gain of +12.5 points after
Whisper STT integration---visual setting search (T4) achieves 29.3\% R@1,
and dialogue grounding (T3) reaches 5.6\% R@1, identifying a dedicated
dialogue index as the primary target for future improvement.  The CLIP visual
index achieves 50.4\% R@1 on a 94\,779-vector benchmark, motivating the
fusion index as a complementary visual-semantic retrieval signal.

The principal contribution of \system\ is a demonstration that scene-level
video understanding can be effectively addressed through structured multi-modal
retrieval at scale, without requiring end-to-end training of a large unified
model.  The modular L0--L5 schema, the independently optimizable multi-tier
indexing architecture, and the complete evaluation suite provide a reproducible
foundation for future research in multi-modal video RAG\@.
